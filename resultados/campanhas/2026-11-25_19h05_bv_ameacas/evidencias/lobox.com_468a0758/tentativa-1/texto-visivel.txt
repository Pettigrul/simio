What Does an Accountant Do? Duties, Types and a Typical Day
An accountant records, checks and explains an organization's money. In practice, that means recording transactions, reconciling accounts, closing the books each month, preparing financial statements and tax returns, and telling managers what the numbers mean for their decisions. So what does an accountant do on an ordinary Tuesday? It depends on the type of accountant, the employer and the time of year. This guide covers the daily work, the main types, how duties change as you move up, and a simple way to tell whether the job would suit you. What does an accountant do day to day? Most accountants spend their days in spreadsheets and accounting software, matching records against bank statements and invoices, fixing differences, posting journal entries and preparing reports. They also answer questions from managers, clients or auditors. The exact mix depends on whether they work in tax, audit, corporate finance or a small firm. If you are reading accountant job details in a listing, these core duties will appear in almost every one: Recording transactions. Entering sales, purchases, payroll and expenses in the general ledger, or reviewing entries that software has posted automatically. Reconciling accounts. Checking that the ledger matches bank statements, credit card statements and supplier records, then finding out why it does not. Month-end and year-end close. Making adjustments, accruals and corrections so the books are complete for the period. Preparing financial statements. Balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement. Tax work. Preparing returns, estimating tax due and keeping records ready for tax authorities. Budgets and forecasts. Comparing actual spending to the plan and explaining the gaps. Supporting audits. Pulling documents and answering auditors' questions. Advising. Telling a manager or business owner what the numbers mean before they hire, buy or cut costs. Accountant vs bookkeeper vs auditor These three roles overlap, which is why people mix them up. The simplest way to separate them is by what each one is responsible for. In small businesses, one person often does both the bookkeeping and the accounting. In larger companies, the roles are split. Types of accountants and what they do Accounting is several jobs under one title. Here are the main paths you will see in job listings. If you are still deciding, public accounting and corporate (management) accounting are the two most common starting points. What accountants do at each career level Your duties change a lot as you gain experience. Early on you do the detailed work. Later you review other people's work and spend more time explaining numbers to leadership. What does a staff accountant do? A staff accountant handles the detailed, recurring work that keeps the books accurate. That includes posting journal entries, reconciling bank and balance sheet accounts, processing or reviewing invoices, and completing assigned month-end tasks. It is the most common entry-level accounting job and where most people learn how a company's books really work. Senior accountant job duties A senior accountant does fewer routine entries and more checking. Typical duties include reviewing staff reconciliations, preparing financial statements, handling complex transactions such as accruals and fixed assets, preparing audit schedules and training newer team members. Many seniors also improve processes, such as cutting days off the month-end close. A sample week for a staff accountant This is an illustration of how a week near month-end often looks in a corporate accounting team. Your week will vary by employer. In mid-month weeks, the pace is usually calmer. That is when accountants work on projects, clean up old balances or help with budgets. What is busy season really like? Workload in accounting comes in waves. In public accounting, the busiest period is usually the run-up to the main tax filing deadline and year-end audits, when longer hours are common. In corporate roles, the pressure is shorter and more regular: a few intense days at every month-end close, and a heavier stretch at year-end. If you want steady, predictable hours, ask about busy season and month-end expectations in every interview. Answers vary a lot between employers. Where do accountants work? Accountants work almost everywhere money moves. The most common employers are: Public accounting firms, from small local practices to large international firms Companies in every industry, in their finance or accounting department Government agencies at federal, state and local level Nonprofits, schools and hospitals Self-employment, serving small businesses and individuals Many roles are now hybrid or remote, especially after the first year or two. Entry-level roles are more often in the office, because that is where you learn fastest. Skills and tools accountants use The job needs more than being good with numbers. The skills employers ask for most often fall into two groups. Hard skills Spreadsheets: lookups, pivot tables, formulas and tidy formatting Accounting software, such as QuickBooks or Xero in small businesses and larger ERP systems such as NetSuite, SAP or Oracle in bigger companies Understanding of accounting principles (GAAP in the US) Reconciliation and attention to detail Soft skills Explaining numbers in plain language to people who are not accountants Chasing information from other teams politely and persistently Managing deadlines when several closes or clients overlap Honesty and judgment, because you often see sensitive information If you are building a resume for accounting roles, our guide to the best skills to put on a resume shows how to list these so recruiters notice them. Do you need a CPA to be an accountant? No. Many accountants work without a CPA license, especially in corporate roles. A bachelor's degree in accounting or a related field is the usual entry requirement. A CPA becomes important if you want to sign audit opinions, move up in public accounting or reach senior finance roles. Requirements are set by each state. Most states require a set amount of college credit, passing the CPA exam and relevant work experience before you can be licensed. Other certifications, such as the CMA for management accountants, can also help depending on the path you choose. How much do accountants earn? Pay depends on location, industry, level and certifications. For current US figures, check the Accountants and Auditors page in the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, which lists median pay and the job outlook. For pay in your city, compare recent job listings in your area. Is accounting the right career for you? Accounting tends to suit people who enjoy order, finishing tasks completely and finding out why two numbers do not match. It is a weaker fit if you dislike routine or deadlines. A quick self-test helps. Answer honestly: Do you get satisfaction from making something add up exactly? Can you handle a few weeks a year of long hours if the rest of the year is steadier? Are you comfortable asking colleagues for missing documents, more than once? Would you enjoy explaining numbers to people who find them confusing? Are you willing to keep learning as tax rules and software change? If you said yes to most, accounting is worth a closer look. The best next step is to speak with people already doing the job. Two or three short conversations with working accountants will tell you more about busy season, culture and career paths than any article. Public accounting if you want fast learning and a clear route to a CPA. Corporate accounting if you want steadier hours. Get the basics. A degree in accounting is the standard route. Career changers often start with accounting coursework or an entry-level role such as accounts payable or bookkeeping. Build practical skills. Get comfortable with spreadsheets and at least one accounting software package. Write a targeted resume. Coming from another field? For a small business, an accountant usually keeps the books accurate, prepares tax returns, files on time and explains how the business is doing. Many also help with budgets, payroll questions and decisions such as whether you can afford to hire. Some owners use an accountant monthly, others only at tax time. 2. Is being an accountant stressful? It can be, mostly around deadlines. Month-end close, year-end and tax season bring pressure and sometimes long hours. Outside those periods, many accountants describe the work as steady and predictable. Stress levels vary a lot by employer, so ask about busy season and team size before you accept an offer. 3. What is the difference between an accountant and a CPA? An accountant is anyone working in accounting. A CPA is an accountant who has met their state's education and experience rules and passed the CPA exam. Only licensed CPAs can sign audit opinions, and the license often helps with promotion in public accounting and senior finance roles. 4. Do accountants only work with taxes? No. Tax is one specialty. Many accountants never prepare a tax return. They work on monthly closes, financial statements, budgets, cost analysis, audits or fraud investigations. If tax work does not appeal to you, management accounting, internal audit or corporate accounting are common alternatives. 5. Can I become an accountant without an accounting degree? Yes, though the path is longer. Career changers often start in accounts payable, bookkeeping or an accounting assistant role, take accounting courses, and move up with experience. A CPA license has formal education requirements, so check your state board's rules if that is your long-term goal. 6. What does a staff accountant do in their first year? In the first year, a staff accountant mostly learns the company's close process. Expect reconciliations, journal entries, invoice reviews and supporting the senior accountant. You will make corrections, learn the software and gradually take ownership of a set of accounts each month.

Applicant Tracking System Resume: Rules + Free Template
Applicant Tracking System Resume: Format Rules and a Free ATS-Friendly Template An applicant tracking system resume is a resume formatted so hiring software can read it correctly: one column, standard section headings, plain fonts, contact details in the main body, and keywords taken from the job post. Get those right and your resume lands in the recruiter's queue looking the way you wrote it. Below you'll find a free ATS-friendly resume template in Word, the formatting rules that matter, a filled example, and a two-minute test you can run on your current resume before you hit apply. What is an applicant tracking system resume? An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software employers use to collect applications, store candidate profiles and move people through hiring stages. An applicant tracking system resume is simply one the software can parse into the right fields (name, job titles, dates, skills) without scrambling the text, so a recruiter can find and read it. The parsing part is the important bit. When you upload a file, the ATS pulls the text out and sorts it into a candidate record. If your layout confuses it, your job title might end up in the wrong field or your contact details might go missing. The recruiter then sees a messy profile, or doesn't find you in a keyword search at all. Do applicant tracking systems reject resumes automatically? Mostly no. In an Enhancv study of 25 US recruiters (September to October 2025), 23 said their ATS does not auto-reject resumes. A person still makes the call. The automatic rejections that do happen usually come from knockout questions, such as work authorization, a required license or location. You've probably seen the claim that 75% of resumes are rejected by software before a human looks at them. It gets repeated everywhere, usually with no study behind it. The recruiters in that study pointed to something more ordinary: volume. A popular role can attract far more applicants than anyone can read closely, so recruiters search and filter. That changes what you should focus on: • Readability. If the ATS parses your resume badly, a recruiter skimming the profile sees a mess. • Searchability. Recruiters search candidate databases by job title and skill. If your resume never uses the words they search for, you don't show up. • Knockout questions. Answer them honestly and carefully. A wrong click on "Are you authorized to work in the US?" does more damage than any font choice. Some ATS platforms also show AI match or fit scores. In the same study, 11 of the 25 recruiters had them, and most used the score as a guide rather than a decision. Free ATS-friendly resume template (Word) [Download the free ATS-friendly resume template (.docx)] This template is built for parsing first and looks clean enough for the human who reads it next. It's a Word file, so it opens in Microsoft Word, Google Docs and LibreOffice. What's inside: • One column, no tables, text boxes, icons or graphics • Contact details in the body of the page, not in the header • Standard headings: Summary, Skills, Work Experience, Education, Certifications • Word's built-in bullet points and heading styles • Calibri 11 pt body text with simple bold for job titles • Dates written the same way throughout ("Mar 2024 - Present") Here's the structure, so you can see it before you download: [FIRST NAME LAST NAME] [Target Job Title, copied from the job post] [City, State] | [Phone] | [email@example.com] | [Profile URL] SUMMARY [Job title] with [X] years of experience in [2 to 3 core areas from the job post]. Known for [one specific strength backed by a result]. SKILLS [Hard skill 1], [Hard skill 2], [Software or tool], [Certification or method] WORK EXPERIENCE [Job Title] [Company Name] | [City, State or Remote] | [Mon YYYY] - Present • [Action verb] + [what you did] + [result with a number, if you have one] • [Action verb] + [a responsibility that matches a keyword in the job post] EDUCATION [Degree, Major] [School Name] | [City, State] | [YYYY] CERTIFICATIONS [Certification Name], [Issuing Organization], [YYYY] Replace every bracket, delete sections you don't need, and keep it to one or two pages. ATS resume formatting rules that matter The applicant tracking system resume format comes down to one idea: make it easy for software to pull your text out in the right order. Here's what to do and what to avoid. Layout: keep it to one column Some well-built two-column templates parse fine. The trouble is you can't know which system each employer uses, so a single column is the safe default. It also reads well on a phone, which is where plenty of recruiters skim. Section headings: use the boring names Creative headings look nice and parse badly. "Work Experience" or "Professional Experience" tells the software exactly what follows. Save the personality for your summary and bullet points. Contact details: put them in the body Type your name, phone, email, city and profile link as normal text at the top of the page. Don't tuck them into Word's header area. Leave out your full street address; city and state are enough. File name: make it easy to find Name the file "Firstname-Lastname-Resume.docx" or add the job title. "resume_final_v3.docx" doesn't help anyone. Which sections should an ATS-friendly resume include? Use this order for most roles: 1. Contact details. Name, target job title, city and state, phone, email, profile link. 2. Summary. Two or three lines on who you are and what you bring. Our resume summary examples show how to write one fast. 3. Skills. Hard skills, tools and certifications that match the job post. See our list of skills to put on a resume if you're stuck. 4. Work experience. Reverse chronological order, newest first. 5. Education. Degree, school, year (the year is optional if it was a long time ago). 6. Certifications, projects or volunteer work. Only if they support the role. If you're early in your career, an objective statement can replace the summary. If you're switching fields, our career change resume guide covers how to lead with transferable skills while keeping the same ATS-safe layout. How to add keywords from the job post Keywords are the job titles, skills, tools and certifications a recruiter is likely to search for. You find them in the job post itself. 1. Copy the job post into a document. Highlight hard skills, tools, certifications and the exact job title. 2. Mark the repeats. Words that show up in the title, the requirements and the responsibilities are the ones that matter most. 3. Match the employer's wording where it's true. If they say "customer success" and you wrote "client support", use their phrase if it honestly describes your work. 4. Put keywords where they make sense. Your title line, summary, skills section and bullet points. A skill shown in a real bullet carries more weight with the human reader than a skill only listed. 5. Include both forms of acronyms once. "Search engine optimization (SEO)" covers both ways someone might search. 6. Leave out what you can't back up. If a recruiter asks about it in the interview, you need a real example. Don't paste the job description in white text or repeat a keyword ten times. Recruiters see the parsed text, and stuffing makes you look worse, not better. Applying to several similar roles? Keep a master resume and tailor a copy for each application. Browsing a batch of open roles on Lobox is a quick way to spot the skills and job titles that keep coming up in your field, so you know which keywords belong in your master version. Applicant tracking system resume example Here's a filled example for a customer success role. Every detail is fictional; copy the structure, not the content. JORDAN REYES Customer Success Manager Austin, TX | (555) 010-2468 | jordan.reyes@email.com | [profile URL] SUMMARY Customer success manager with 5 years of experience in B2B SaaS onboarding, account renewals and customer training. Known for turning at-risk accounts into renewals through structured check-ins and clear success plans. SKILLS Customer onboarding, Account management, Renewals and upsells, Salesforce, Zendesk, Gainsight, QBR planning, Customer training, Churn analysis WORK EXPERIENCE Customer Success Manager Brightline Software | Austin, TX | Mar 2022 - Present • Manage a portfolio of 60 mid-market accounts, leading onboarding, quarterly business reviews and renewal planning • Built a 30-day onboarding checklist used by the whole CS team • Partner with sales on upsell opportunities identified during QBRs Customer Support Specialist Northfield Apps | Remote | Jun 2019 - Feb 2022 • Resolved technical and billing tickets in Zendesk across email and chat • Wrote 40+ help center articles on common setup questions EDUCATION B.A., Communication University of Texas at Austin | Austin, TX | 2019 Notice what it does: the exact job title sits under the name, the skills line mirrors a typical customer success job post, and the keywords show up again inside real bullet points. How to test your resume before you apply You don't need a paid tool to run a basic applicant tracking system resume test. Try this: 1. Copy and paste test. Open your file, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor (Notepad, TextEdit in plain text mode). Read it top to bottom. 2. Check the order. Your name should come first, then contact details, then sections in order. If lines from two columns are mixed together, that's what a parser may see. 3. Check for missing text. If your email, phone or job titles are gone, they were probably in a header, text box or graphic. 4. Check for strange characters. Boxes, question marks or broken symbols mean a font or bullet didn't convert. 5. PDF users: try selecting the text. If you can't highlight words in your PDF, it's an image and an ATS can't read it. Free and paid applicant tracking system resume check tools can add a keyword comparison against a job post. Treat the score as a hint, not a pass or fail. No checker knows how a specific employer has set up its system. Pre-submit checklist • One column, no tables, text boxes, icons or photos • Name and contact details in the body, not the header • Standard section headings • Exact job title from the post near the top • Keywords from the job post in the summary, skills and bullets • Same date format everywhere • File type matches what the job post asks for • File named with your name • Plain text paste test done • Knockout questions answered carefully Ready to apply? How to Write a Cover Letter (Step-by-Step Guide with Template)
To write a cover letter, pick the three things the job post asks for most, then show in under a page how you've already done them. Open with the role and your strongest match, back it up with one or two specific examples, and close by asking for a conversation. That's the whole method. Below you'll find how to write a cover letter for a job step by step, a template you can copy, two examples, and a checklist to run before you hit send. What is a cover letter for a job? A cover letter is a short letter, usually one page, that you send with your resume when you apply for a job. Your resume lists what you've done. The cover letter explains why that experience fits this specific role and this specific company, in your own voice. It's your chance to connect the dots for the hiring manager. Not every employer reads them, and some applications make them optional. Write one anyway when the job matters to you. A tailored letter is one of the few parts of an application where you control the story. How to write a cover letter in 6 steps Step 1: Pull three requirements from the job post Before you write a word, open the job description and highlight what comes up most. Look at the first few bullet points, anything repeated, and anything marked "required". Pick the top three. For each, note one moment from your work where you did that thing and what happened as a result. Those three pairs are the raw material for your whole letter. Step 2: Write the header Keep it simple and match the look of your resume. Include: • Your full name • Phone number and professional email • City and state (a full street address is optional) • A link to your portfolio or professional profile, if relevant • The date • The hiring manager's name, title and company If you're pasting the letter into an online form or an email, skip the date and the employer's address block. Put your contact details under your signature instead. Step 3: Address it to a real person The best way to address a cover letter is by name: "Dear Priya Sharma" or "Dear Ms. Sharma". If you don't know their title or pronouns, the full name is the safe choice. To find the name, check the job post, the company's team page, or professional networks. If you still can't find it, use a specific team greeting: • Dear Hiring Manager • Dear Marketing Team • Dear [Company Name] Recruiting Team Avoid "To whom it may concern". It reads like a form letter, which is the opposite of what you want. Step 4: Open with the job and your strongest match Your first two sentences decide whether the rest gets read. Name the role, then lead with the single best reason you fit. Skip "My name is" and "I am writing to apply". They already know. Weak: "I am writing to express my interest in the Account Manager position at your company." Better: "I'm applying for the Account Manager role at Brightline because I've spent three years doing exactly what your post describes: keeping a large book of B2B clients happy and renewing." If someone referred you, mention them here. It's the strongest opener you can have. Step 5: Prove it in one or two body paragraphs This is where your three pairs from Step 1 go. Write one short paragraph per point, or group them into two paragraphs if they're related. Use this simple pattern for each one: 1. The need: what the company wants 2. The proof: what you did, with a number or clear result if you have one 3. The link: how that helps them in this role Don't repeat your resume line by line. Pick the stories behind the bullet points. Add one sentence about why this company specifically, based on something real: a product you use, a recent launch, or how they describe their team. Step 6: End with a clear next step To end a cover letter well, sum up your fit in one line, thank them, and invite a conversation. Then sign off. "I'd welcome the chance to talk about how I could help your team keep renewals on track. Thank you for your time, and I look forward to hearing from you." Good sign-offs: "Best regards", "Kind regards", "Sincerely". Then your full name. Avoid casual closers like "Cheers" unless the company's tone is clearly informal. For businesses operating in competitive markets, the quality of customer support can directly influence customer satisfaction, brand reputation, and business growth. However, excellent customer service does not happen automatically. Employees need proper training, practical experience, and a clear understanding of customer expectations. Without the right skills, even experienced employees may struggle to handle difficult conversations, resolve complaints, or provide consistent support. Customer service training helps employees communicate effectively, understand customer needs, solve problems, and represent a company professionally. Whether a business operates through physical stores, websites, mobile applications, or social media, investing in employee development is essential. Let's explore 12 important customer service training topics every organization should include in its training program. 1. Effective Communication Skills Communication is the foundation of excellent customer service. Employees interact with customers through phone calls, emails, live chats, and face-to-face conversations. Each interaction requires clarity, professionalism, and appropriate language. Training should focus on clear verbal and written communication, professional email and chat responses, appropriate tone and language, asking relevant questions, and explaining complicated information simply. Practical example: When a customer complains about a delayed delivery, an employee should acknowledge the concern, explain the reason for the delay, and communicate the expected delivery date. Good communication reduces misunderstandings and creates a more positive customer experience. 2. Understanding Customer Needs and Expectations Every customer has different expectations. Some want quick answers, while others need detailed explanations before making a decision. Customer service representatives should learn how to identify customer requirements instead of immediately offering generic solutions. Training should include understanding customer behavior, asking relevant questions, identifying problems, and confirming customer expectations. For example, if a customer contacts a software company because an application is not working, the representative should first understand the problem, device, and circumstances before suggesting a solution. Understanding customer needs helps employees provide relevant and personalized assistance. 3. Active Listening and Empathy Active listening means giving customers full attention and understanding their concerns without interrupting them. Empathy means recognizing how customers feel and responding with understanding and respect. Employees should learn to listen without interrupting, avoid assumptions, acknowledge customer concerns, ask questions for clarification, and respond calmly. Example: Saying “I understand how frustrating this situation must be. Let me help you resolve it” can make a difficult interaction more positive. Empathy helps build trust and makes customers feel valued. 4. Problem-Solving and Complaint Handling Resolving customer problems is one of the most important responsibilities of a customer service team. Employees must know how to identify issues, investigate their causes, explain possible solutions, and follow up when necessary. Common situations covered during training include delayed deliveries, incorrect billing information, refund and cancellation requests, product defects, technical difficulties, and poor service experiences. A structured problem-solving approach helps teams resolve issues efficiently while maintaining professionalism. 5. Product and Service Knowledge Employees cannot provide accurate assistance without understanding the products or services they represent. Product knowledge training should cover features, benefits, pricing, usage instructions, company policies, limitations, and frequently asked questions. For example, a customer support employee working for an e-commerce company should understand order tracking, payment methods, return policies, and refund procedures. Strong product knowledge increases employee confidence and helps customers receive accurate information. 6. Time Management and Response Handling Customers expect timely responses, especially when dealing with urgent problems. Customer service teams must learn how to manage multiple requests without compromising service quality. Important training areas include prioritizing urgent customer issues, managing multiple conversations, following response-time guidelines, organizing support tickets, completing customer follow-ups, and maintaining accuracy while working efficiently. Employees should understand that faster responses are valuable, but providing incorrect information just to save time can create additional problems. 7. Customer Relationship Management (CRM) Tools Customer Relationship Management (CRM) software helps businesses organize customer information, track interactions, manage support requests, and maintain communication history. Customer service employees should receive practical training on the CRM systems used by their organization. Important CRM skills include creating and updating customer records, managing support tickets, tracking previous interactions, assigning requests to relevant departments, scheduling follow-ups, and generating customer service reports. CRM knowledge helps employees provide consistent support and reduces the need for customers to repeat information. 8. Handling Difficult and Angry Customers Not every customer interaction is easy. Some customers may be frustrated because of delayed services, unexpected charges, technical issues, or unmet expectations. Employees should learn how to remain calm, avoid arguments, and focus on finding practical solutions. Training should cover conflict management, professional language, emotional control, escalation procedures, and complaint resolution. An important principle is to focus on resolving the customer's problem rather than proving who is right. If an issue cannot be resolved at the employee level, it should be escalated to the appropriate supervisor. 9. Data Privacy and Customer Security Customer service representatives often handle personal details, contact information, account records, and transaction-related information. Protecting this information is essential for maintaining customer trust and following organizational security policies. Training should include password protection, customer identity verification, secure communication practices, confidential information handling, access permissions, and recognizing suspicious requests. Employees should never disclose customer information without proper authorization. A strong understanding of data privacy helps reduce security risks and protects both customers and businesses. 10. The structure of a good sales pitch Almost every pitch that works, whether it's a cold email or a two-minute talk at an event, follows these five parts. Keep the proof honest. A real customer result, a named client (with permission) or a short demo beats a big claim every time. If you don't have a result yet, offer a trial or a sample instead of inventing a number. How to write a sales pitch in 6 steps 1. Pick one buyer and one problem A pitch written for everyone persuades no one. Before you write a word, decide who exactly you're talking to (role, company size, situation) and the single problem you'll lead with. A finance director and an office manager at the same company need different pitches, even for the same product. 2. Research before you write Spend five minutes on the person and their company. Check their profile, recent posts, company news, job openings and the tools they already use. Job openings are a strong signal: a company hiring three new sales reps probably has an onboarding or pipeline problem worth talking about. This is where a professional networking platform helps. When you're connected with people in your target industry, you see their updates, job changes and company news without digging. You can connect with professionals and recruiters on Lobox to keep that research close at hand. 3. Open with them, not you Your first line decides whether the rest gets read. Skip "My name is" and "I'm reaching out because". Open with something about their world: a recent change, a shared connection, or a problem common to their role. Weak: "Hi, I'm Sam from BrightDesk, a leading helpdesk platform." Better: "Your support team posted three job openings this month. That usually means tickets are piling up." 4. Show the change, then prove it Buyers don't buy features. They buy the difference between how things work now and how they could work. Describe that change in one or two sentences, then back it up with one piece of proof. One strong proof point is more convincing than five weak ones. 5. Ask for one small next step End with a single ask that's easy to say yes to. "Can we do a 15-minute call on Tuesday or Wednesday?" works better than "Let me know your thoughts" or "Would you like to buy?" Give a specific action and, where you can, a specific time. 6. Cut it, then say it out loud Read your pitch aloud. If you run out of breath, it's too long. Delete any sentence the buyer wouldn't miss, any adjective that doesn't add meaning, and any line that talks about your company instead of their problem. Most first drafts get better when you cut them by a third. Types of sales pitch The structure stays the same, but the length and tone change with the channel. Here are the main types and when to use each. The word counts and timings above are working guidelines from common sales practice, not hard rules. Test what gets replies in your market. Common sales pitch mistakes and how to fix them How long should a sales pitch be? A sales pitch should be as short as it can be while still covering the buyer's problem, your solution, one proof point and an ask. In practice that means roughly 50 to 125 words for a cold email, about 30 seconds for a cold call opener or elevator pitch, and longer only in a booked meeting where the buyer expects detail. If you're unsure, go shorter. A buyer who's interested will ask for more. A buyer who's overloaded will skip a long message entirely. What makes a sales pitch effective? An effective sales pitch is specific to one buyer, focused on a problem they already feel, honest in its proof and easy to respond to. It sounds like a person talking, not a brochure. The biggest difference between pitches that get replies and ones that don't is usually the first line: it either shows you understand the buyer or it doesn't. AI Recruiting: What It Can and Cannot Do for Hiring
AI Recruiting: What It Can and Cannot Do for Your Hiring AI recruiting is the use of AI software to handle parts of hiring: finding candidates, screening resumes, drafting job posts and messages, scheduling interviews and answering applicant questions. It's good at fast, repetitive work at volume. It's weak at judgment and context, and it can repeat bias from past data. Use it to cut admin time, and keep a person making every hiring decision. This guide is for owners and hiring managers who don't have a full recruiting team. You'll see what AI handles well, where it falls short, whether it's worth paying for at your hiring volume, and what to ask a vendor before you sign. What is AI recruiting? AI recruiting means using AI-powered software anywhere in the hiring process, from sourcing and screening to scheduling and candidate messages. The software reads resumes, profiles and job requirements, spots patterns, then sorts, ranks, drafts or replies faster than a person can. It supports the people hiring. It doesn't replace the person who decides who gets the job. Most of what you'll come across fits one of three groups: • AI features inside software you already use, such as an applicant tracking system (ATS), a job board or an HR platform • Standalone AI recruiting tools built for one job, like sourcing, screening or scheduling • General AI assistants you prompt yourself, mostly for writing job posts, emails and interview questions One warning: "AI-powered" covers everything from a basic keyword filter to software that ranks candidates on its own. When a vendor says AI, ask what the feature actually does and what it decides. What an AI recruiting tool can do well An AI recruiting tool is most useful on high-volume, repeatable tasks: drafting job posts and outreach, searching large candidate pools, sorting applications against clear requirements, booking interviews and answering routine applicant questions. The common thread is speed on work with clear rules. Anything that needs judgment about a person still needs you. AI recruiting assistant: the admin layer An AI recruiting assistant usually handles coordination: booking interviews, sending reminders, updating candidates on where they stand and drafting follow-ups. For a small team, this is where you get the most time back with the least risk, because the tool isn't deciding who moves forward. If you only try one thing, try this. Candidates also notice it. A same-day reply with three interview slots feels very different from a week of silence. Sourcing and matching Matching tools compare a candidate's skills and experience with what your job asks for, across far more profiles than you could read. They're fast. But they only look for what you tell them to, using the words people happen to put on their profiles. Someone who describes the same skill in different words can slip off the list. That's why sourcing shouldn't start and end inside one tool. Referrals and your own network still find people that ranking misses. Posting your role and connecting with professionals directly on a platform like Lobox widens the pool before any filter touches it. What AI recruiting cannot do AI recruiting cannot judge whether someone will do well in your team, spot potential in people with unusual backgrounds, guarantee fair results or carry legal responsibility for your decisions. It can't fix a vague job description or convince a strong candidate to choose you. Those parts of hiring stay with you. It can't judge fit or potential AI ranks people by how closely their resume matches the job and how similar they look to past patterns. That works against career changers, people returning to work and anyone self-taught. Take a former teacher applying for a training coordinator role. Her resume says "lesson planning" and "classroom management", not "instructional design" or "learning programs". A keyword match ranks her low. A person reading it sees someone who has designed and delivered training every working day for years. It can't guarantee fair outcomes AI learns from data. If your past hires leaned one way, by age, gender or background, a tool trained on that history can learn the same lean and repeat it at scale. Amazon reportedly dropped an experimental resume-screening tool after finding it marked down resumes linked to women Courts are now looking at the software itself. In Mobley v. Workday, filed in 2023, a job applicant alleged that Workday's screening technology discriminated on the basis of age, race and disability. In June 2026 a federal judge refused Workday's request to dismiss the California state-law claims (SHRM). The case hasn't been decided, but the message for employers is clear: "the software made the call" won't protect you. It can't keep you compliant on its own The rules depend on where you hire. Two US examples: • New York City: Local Law 144 covers automated employment decision tools. Employers using one need an independent bias audit every year, must publish a summary of the results and must tell candidates who live in the city that a tool is being used (NYC DCWP FAQ). • Colorado: a new law, SB 26-189, replaced the state's 2024 AI Act. It focuses on notice, disclosure, recordkeeping and human review, and takes effect January 1, 2027 (Proskauer). Federal anti-discrimination law applies whether or not a tool is involved. This isn't legal advice. If you hire in several states, check with an employment lawyer before you switch on automated screening. It can't fix a bad job description If your requirements are wrong or padded, AI filters on the wrong things, just faster. Ten "required" items that are really nice-to-haves will screen out good people at scale. Get the role right first with a clear job description, then let a tool sort against it. It can't build the relationship Candidates are choosing you too, and the best ones usually have options. A real conversation with the hiring manager does more to win someone over than a perfectly timed automated email. Use AI to clear the admin so you have time for those conversations. Is AI recruiting worth it for a small business? It's worth it when you get more applications than you can read properly, or you hire for the same role again and again. If you hire a handful of people a year and can read every application yourself, a paid tool usually costs more in money and setup than it saves. Start with the AI features already built into tools you use. For the full process around these decisions, see our guide on how to hire employees for a small business. How to use AI in recruiting without losing control 1. Start with the lowest-risk tasks. Drafting and scheduling first. Screening last, once you trust your criteria. 2. Write your criteria before any screening. Split must-haves from nice-to-haves. A tool can only be as clear as you are. 3. Keep a person on every rejection. If that's not realistic, review a sample of the AI's rejects every week. 4. Tell candidates when AI is involved. Some places require it, and it builds trust everywhere. 5. Run structured interviews. Ask every candidate the same core questions so an AI summary never becomes the decision. Our list of interview questions to ask candidates gives you a ready set. 6. Check the results. After a few hires, compare who the tool ranked highly with who actually did well. If they don't line up, change the criteria or drop the tool. Questions to ask before you buy an AI recruiting tool Use this checklist on any demo call: ☐ What exactly does the AI do, and what does it decide or recommend? ☐ Can I see why a candidate was ranked high or low? ☐ Can I switch off automatic rejections? ☐ Has the tool had an independent bias audit, and can I see the summary? ☐ Does it use my candidates' data to train its models? ☐ Where is candidate data stored, and what happens to it if I cancel? ☐ Does it support the candidate notices required where I hire? ☐ What does it cost at my hiring volume, not the vendor's typical customer's? For the wider buying process, including features and pricing models, read how to choose recruitment software. AI recruiting chatbots: helpful or annoying? An AI recruiting chatbot answers applicant questions at any hour: what the role pays, where it's based, what happens next. Done well, it saves candidates from waiting days for a simple answer. Done badly, it traps people in loops and makes your company feel cold. Three rules keep it on the helpful side: • Always offer an easy way to reach a person • Only let it give answers you've approved • Never let it reject anyone Where to start this week Pick one admin task that eats your time, such as scheduling or first-draft job posts, and try AI there. Keep screening and decisions in human hands until you've seen how the tool behaves with your roles. Hiring now? Post your role and connect with professionals and recruiters on Lobox, so your shortlist starts with real people, not just a ranking. FAQ Will AI replace recruiters? No. AI takes over repetitive parts of the job, like scheduling, first-pass sorting and drafting messages. It doesn't replace judging people, building relationships with candidates or negotiating offers. The recruiter's job shifts toward those human parts. For small businesses, the realistic gain is getting a few hours back each week, not removing anyone from the hiring process. Is it legal to use AI to screen resumes? In the US, generally yes, but you stay responsible for the outcome. Anti-discrimination law applies whether a person or software makes the screen. Some places add rules: New York City requires an annual independent bias audit and candidate notices, and Colorado's new law takes effect in January 2027. Check the rules where your candidates live before automating any screening. What is an AI recruiting chatbot? It's an automated assistant on your careers page or job ad that answers applicant questions, collects basic details and books interviews. It's useful for routine questions candidates would otherwise wait days to get answered. Keep its answers approved, give people a clear way to reach a human, and don't let it make rejection decisions. Can AI recruiting tools be biased? Yes. A tool trained on past hiring data can learn the patterns in that data, including unfair ones, and repeat them across every application. Bias can also come from how you set the criteria. Ask vendors for independent bias audit results, review a sample of rejected candidates yourself and compare the tool's rankings with how hires actually perform. How much do AI recruiting tools cost? Pricing varies widely. Some charge per user, some per job posting, some per hire, and many AI features come bundled inside an ATS or HR platform you may already pay for. Ask for a quote based on your actual hiring volume, check what's included in the base plan, and try any built-in features before buying a separate tool. Do candidates know when AI screens them? Not always. Unless a law requires notice, many employers don't say. That's changing: New York City already requires employers to notify candidates who live in the city, and Colorado's law focuses on disclosure and notice. Telling candidates up front, even where it isn't required, is the simplest way to avoid looking like you're hiding something.

AI Entry-Level Jobs: Report Finds 1.5M Missing White-Collar Roles
Nov. 25, 2026 AI Entry-Level Jobs: Report Finds 1.5M Missing White-Collar Roles Industries that use AI the most are hiring fewer beginners, according to a new report from the Burning Glass Institute and RAISE US released on Monday. The report, called "Fewer Rungs, New Bridges," finds that white-collar industries now employ about 1.5 million fewer people than pre-pandemic trends would predict. But the story is not mass layoffs. In the authors' words, the missing workers "aren't being laid off; they're simply not being hired in the first place." What the report found about entry-level hiring The researchers looked at finance, insurance, information and professional services. Together these sectors employed 31.9 million people in August 2026, about 1.5 million short of where their growth from 2005 to 2019 would have put them. Hiring has slowed in these industries even as their output keeps growing. In the most AI-intensive industries, the share of job postings open to people with three years of experience or less was 3% to 8% below its 2021-2026 average in the year ending June 2026. Across the wider economy, that share barely moved. The authors are careful about cause. The 1.5 million figure is a net number that also reflects other forces, such as offshoring and older technology. They treat AI as one of several likely factors, not the only one. Which jobs are shrinking, and which are growing? The split is the most useful part of the report. Between 2023 and 2025, occupations most at risk of automation lost about 820,000 jobs. Over the same period, occupations where AI helps workers do more gained about 1.4 million. Office and administrative support took the biggest hit, along with clerical and customer service roles. Workers without a four-year degree are the most exposed. They hold 80% of the jobs most likely to be automated, compared with 64% of all jobs. And most proven paths from no-degree jobs into better-paid careers still end in roles that usually require a degree. Gina Raimondo, former U.S. Commerce Secretary and co-chair and CEO of RAISE US, called the findings a wake-up call for leaders "from Washington to Silicon Valley." What this means if you're looking for your first job The report's message for job seekers is that the old route, where you learn on the job in a junior role, is getting harder to find. The answer is to bring some of that experience with you. The report points to a few ways to do that: Get real-world experience early. Co-ops, apprenticeships and employer-designed projects can stand in for the training junior jobs used to provide. Learn to use AI on the job. The report urges more focus on applied AI skills, since roles where AI assists workers are the ones growing. Build the human skills. The researchers say communication and collaboration are becoming core skills across the economy, and they help people move into a wider range of jobs. There is some good news for career changers. Looking at 2,741 moves into jobs that employers struggle to fill, the researchers found AI could help newcomers close about 31% of the skills gap. In those cases, AI can work as a bridge into a new field rather than a barrier. Why employers should pay attention Fewer junior hires today means fewer experienced workers and future managers later. If AI takes over the tasks that once trained new staff, companies will need to find other ways to grow their own talent, through apprenticeships, rotations or structured projects. The report adds to a growing body of research on AI and early careers, including a Business Roundtable and Burning Glass briefing published in September. The next official signal is the November jobs report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, due in early December. Watch for hiring trends in finance, professional services and office support roles.

Central Florida Flooding: Timeline for Friday’s Heavy Rain
ORLANDO, Fla. | Nov. 20, 2026 Central Florida flooding is set to get worse Friday as another round of heavy rain moves over ground that is already soaked. Forecasters expect 4 to 8 more inches east of Orlando, with isolated totals above 10 inches, and the heaviest rain arriving Friday morning. The new rain lands on top of what fell from Tuesday through Thursday, when neighborhoods in Brevard and Volusia counties flooded and the St. Johns River broke a record that had stood since 1953. When will the heaviest rain hit? Rain was expected to build late Thursday night, peak Friday and taper off by early Saturday morning. The setup depends on a developing area of low pressure off the South Florida coast. If it tracks closer to Florida, rainfall totals go up. Forecasters at WKMG News 6 said the closer track is the more likely one. FOX 35 meteorologists put Brevard County at 8 to 16 inches through early Saturday, with 4 to 8 inches from New Smyrna Beach into eastern Orange and Osceola counties. Orlando and Sanford are looking at closer to 2 to 4 inches. The system could gain subtropical or tropical traits as it moves north Saturday. Drier weather is expected to return Sunday. Where is flooding worst right now? Brevard County has taken the hardest hit so far. FOX 35 reported more than 100 people rescued there and roughly 21 inches of rain in Melbourne Beach over two days. The American Red Cross opened a shelter in Melbourne. Seminole County has urged people in low-lying areas along the St. Johns River to leave if they can. Schools in Brevard, Volusia and Flagler counties canceled classes Friday. With the ground saturated, even a short burst of heavy rain can cause dangerous flash flooding. Flooded streets can stop even self-driving cars, as San Francisco saw this week when record rain swamped the city. Never drive through standing water, and check weather.gov for the latest National Weather Service alerts for your county. Why the St. Johns River will flood for weeks This is the part of the story that will outlast the storm. The St. Johns River near Cocoa has already passed its 1953 record of 17.6 feet and is still rising. The St. Johns is one of the slowest-flowing rivers in the country. Water drains north toward Jacksonville very slowly, so river levels downstream, near Lake Harney, Sanford and Astor, can keep rising for days after the rain stops. Seminole County Emergency Management has warned flooding could continue through December. That means homeowners along the river should plan for weeks of high water, not just a bad weekend. The heaviest rain falls Friday, with forecasters watching where the low-pressure system tracks. Rain should ease Saturday, then the focus shifts to the St. Johns River, where new crests are expected downstream in the coming days.

Jumbo Stay 747 Hotel Is Being Scrapped at Stockholm Arlanda
STOCKHOLM, Nov. 20, 2026 Jumbo Stay Hotel: Sweden's Famous 747 Hostel Is Being Scrapped The Boeing 747 that served as the Jumbo Stay hotel at Stockholm Arlanda Airport is being dismantled this month. Airport operator Swedavia started removing the jet in November 2026, about 20 months after the quirky hostel closed following bankruptcy. For 16 years, the jumbo jet was the first thing many travellers saw as they drove into Arlanda. Now it is disappearing piece by piece. What happened to the Jumbo Stay hotel? Jumbo Stay shut down in March 2025 after its operator went bankrupt, cancelling more than 800 upcoming bookings. No buyer or new operator came forward, and Swedavia, which owns the land, was left to deal with the aircraft. Swedish local paper Mitt i reported that crews had already removed the engines and cut off the wings. Contractors are now stripping the interior and separating hazardous materials before the fuselage itself is taken apart. Swedish media say the work should be finished by the end of the year. "It's time now," Arlanda airport director Karl Eklund told Swedish Radio's P4 Stockholm. He said the jet had been a good feature at the airport for many years, but recently it hadn't been what you want to see when passing by. Swedavia has not decided what the site will be used for next. The plane had a long life before it became a hotel This wasn't just any 747. The jet entered service with Singapore Airlines in 1976 and later flew for Pan Am, the most famous name in jumbo jet history. Its last operator was Transjet, a Swedish charter airline that went bankrupt in 2002, leaving the plane parked at Arlanda. Swedish entrepreneur Oscar Diös bought it and turned it into Jumbo Hostel, which opened in January 2009. The rebuild gave it 33 rooms and 76 beds. The most popular booking was the cockpit suite, where guests slept beneath the old flight deck windows. Diös told Swedish broadcaster SVT in April 2025 that he hoped the plane wouldn't be cut up for its aluminum. That is now what's happening. Why did Jumbo Stay fail? A novelty hostel had a hard time against large chain hotels. Swedish business outlet Realtid reported that big hotels built right next to Arlanda's terminals took business away from the much smaller Jumbo Stay. Once the hostel went bankrupt, the estate did not have the money to remove the aircraft, so the cost fell to Swedavia. Can you still sleep in a plane? Jumbo Stay was the only hostel you could book inside a real 747, and that option is now gone. Other airplane-themed stays exist around the world, but most use smaller aircraft. In Amsterdam, the Corendon hotel shows off a former KLM 747 on its grounds, but guests can't go inside. Dismantling is expected to wrap up by the end of 2026. After that, Swedavia will decide what to do with the land at the Arlanda entrance.
